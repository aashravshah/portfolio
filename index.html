Fleet Management Platform

Hospitals were losing hours to device failures that the data could have predicted — if only it arrived in time.

The Problem

GE Healthcare's medical devices generate error logs continuously across every hospital they're installed in. But those logs were collected and processed in batch — meaning that when a device threw an error, the right people (biomedical engineers on-site or remote field service engineers, depending on the issue) wouldn't see it for hours.

By the time the data arrived, the window to intervene had often closed. Engineers were diagnosing faults retroactively, managing fallout rather than preventing it. Hospital teams were left dealing with unplanned downtime on equipment that was critical to patient care.

Discovery

I ran discovery with 20+ biomedical engineers across hospital sites to understand where the actual breakdown was. What emerged was consistent: the data they needed existed, it was just reaching them hours too late to be useful. The ask wasn't better analytics — it was faster delivery.

That reframed the brief entirely. This wasn't a monitoring problem or a visualization problem. It was a data delivery problem — and solving it meant pushing logs to the right engineer the moment an error occurred, not hours later in a batch.

Key Decision — Scheduled Batch vs. Event-Triggered Log Transfer

Before: scheduled batch processing

Device logs were collected on a schedule and processed in bulk. Manageable at low volumes, but the delay between error and visibility made it impossible to intervene in time. Engineers were always looking at what had already happened.

After: real-time log transfer API

Built on AWS API Gateway, logs are pushed the moment an error occurs — directly to the biomedical or field service engineer responsible for that device. The right person gets the signal in time to act, before a service call becomes unavoidable.

The Product

The fleet management platform combines real-time log transfer with predictive fault detection — surfacing device health signals to biomedical engineers on-site and remote field service teams, routed by issue type. The result is a platform that moves hospital device support from reactive to proactive: engineers know about problems sooner, can triage remotely where possible, and show up to hospitals prepared rather than starting from scratch on-site.

IoT Streaming Platform

Moving 5TB+ of daily device telemetry from batch to streaming — and everything that became possible once we did.

The Problem

The fleet platform proved real-time data delivery mattered. But the infrastructure underneath it was still fundamentally batch. At 5TB+ per day, that ceiling had consequences everywhere: ML models couldn't run inference on fresh data, remote diagnostics were working with telemetry that was already hours old, and every new data source took roughly four months to onboard because schemas differed across business units and regions with no shared standard.

The platform needed a new foundation — one that could handle the scale, maintain HIPAA compliance without interruption, and stay open to use cases we hadn't fully defined yet.

The Strategy

This was a multi-year roadmap, not a single sprint. The goal was to migrate the core IoT data pipeline from batch to streaming while keeping the HIPAA compliance posture solid throughout — no gaps in the compliance story while the architecture underneath was changing.

The first and most consequential decision was streaming infrastructure: whatever we chose would shape every product built on top of it for years.

Key Decision — Amazon Kinesis vs. MSK (Kafka)

Considered: Amazon Kinesis

AWS-native with lower initial setup overhead. But shard-based pricing becomes difficult to predict at 5TB+/day volumes, and Kinesis's replay window is short — limiting how far back ML teams could reach for training data, debugging, or reprocessing when models needed to be retrained.

Chosen: MSK (Kafka)

More predictable cost at our scale. Kafka's log-based model gives any downstream consumer — ML pipelines, observability tooling, future use cases — the ability to replay historical event streams on demand. We chose it to stay open to a roadmap that wasn't fully written yet, while keeping costs controllable as volumes grew.

What the Platform Unlocked

The streaming foundation made four things possible that batch couldn't support:

ML-based anomaly detection — with real-time telemetry flowing in, we built models to identify critical component failures 84x faster than before, from a mean detection time of 21 hours down to 15 minutes. Field engineers could now intervene before faults escalated to $15K+ replacement events.

Observability across 10+ business modalities — self-serve Kibana dashboards, built on top of the streaming layer, gave 1,000+ design, clinical, and manufacturing engineers a live view of device and platform health without needing to raise a support ticket or wait for a report.

Schema standardization — we consolidated 5+ file formats across business units and regions into a unified pipeline schema, collapsing new data source onboarding from four months to days.

The foundation for the LLM agent — real-time, structured, replay-capable telemetry was the prerequisite for building a conversational AI layer on top. Without this platform, that product wouldn't have been possible.

POC / MVP · AI-Powered Product

LLM-Powered Conversational Agent

Engineers were drowning in logs they didn't have time to read. We built an agent that reads them, reasons over them, and answers back in plain language.

POC & MVP validated — not yet in production
The Problem

The streaming platform made data fast. But fast data is only useful if someone can make sense of it. For a biomedical engineer on a hospital floor, a field service engineer handling multiple sites remotely, or a remote diagnostics specialist reviewing telemetry from another city — the logs themselves were still dense, technical, and time-consuming to work through.

The pattern was the same across all three personas: hours spent reading log files, cross-referencing telemetry, and working toward a conclusion that an experienced engineer would eventually reach — but only after significant manual effort. And once they had a conclusion, there was no easy way to ask a follow-up.

The Insight

The problem wasn't a lack of data. It was a lack of interpretation. The streaming platform was delivering device telemetry and system anomalies in near real time — but the last mile, from structured log to actionable understanding, still required a human expert to close manually.

An LLM layer sitting on top of that telemetry could do that interpretation automatically: analyse the incoming signals, surface the anomaly, explain what it means in plain language, and let the engineer ask follow-up questions without needing to re-read the underlying data.

Key Decision — Generic Prompting vs. Metadata-Based Fine-Tuning

Option A: generic prompting

Prompt an off-the-shelf LLM with raw logs and ask it to reason over them. Fast to prototype, but the model has no context about device types, failure history, or the vocabulary that engineers actually use — leading to generic outputs that miss the specificity the personas need.

Chosen: metadata-based fine-tuning

Ground the model in device-specific metadata — component context, known failure patterns, historical telemetry — so it reasons about this device, this anomaly, this history, not logs in the abstract. The result is descriptive, persona-appropriate output that engineers can act on directly, and ask follow-ups against.

What the Agent Does

The conversational agent connects directly to the streaming telemetry pipeline and device metadata. When an anomaly is detected, the agent surfaces a plain-language explanation — what's happening, what the likely cause is, and what action is typically warranted — routed to the right persona. A biomedical engineer on-site gets a different level of detail than a remote diagnostics specialist reviewing the same event.

Engineers can then interact conversationally: ask follow-up questions about the data, drill into specific time windows, or request a comparison against prior incidents — without needing to leave the interface or open a separate log viewer. The agent handles both the analysis (what does this data mean?) and the inference (what should I do?), so engineers spend their time deciding, not decoding.
