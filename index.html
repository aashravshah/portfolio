01
Case 01 · 0→1 product

Fleet Management Platform

Hospitals lose hours to device failures the data could have flagged, if it had arrived in time.

Live · streaming path launching
The story

Services are a significant part of GE HealthCare’s revenue, but smaller, portable devices (the kind carried from room to room, like anesthesia machines) often had no hospital service contract behind them. I interviewed 20+ biomedical engineers and the real problem came into focus: device logs arrived on a schedule, so by the time an engineer saw a failure, the window to act had closed.

It wasn’t an analytics problem. It was a delivery problem. So I led strategy and roadmap for a fleet management product, built from scratch and sold to hospitals, that lets an engineer get the log they need when they need it.

My role

Led product strategy and roadmap from discovery to launch, delivered in six months. I put the connection to the IoT gateway first, because everything else depended on it, and worked with the IoT team (who didn’t report to me) to build it.

Swipe the diagram sideways →
Fig 01 — Log delivery is live; streaming telemetry reuses the same pipeline

Read it left to right: an engineer asks for a log, it’s fetched from the device and lands in our storage, and secure APIs let engineers search and download it. The dashed lane is the streaming path that is now launching.

Key decision — wait for the log, or fetch it on demand

Before: scheduled delivery

Logs collected on a schedule and processed in bulk. Engineers see what already happened: fine for root cause, too late for prevention.

Chosen: on demand, plus automatic

An engineer requests a log and it’s retrieved from the device; new files are also copied across automatically. Streaming telemetry follows on the same pipeline.

Under the hoodfor the technical reader
On demand
An engineer requests a log. The IoT service retrieves it from the device and publishes an SNS message with the file’s location. Our team reads it from SQS and copies the log into our S3 bucket.
Automatic
An S3 replication rule copies new files arriving in the IoT bucket into ours.
Secure APIs
One API searches logs by date range and system ID. A second retrieves the selected log by its ETag and system ID so the engineer can download it.
Streaming (launching)
Telemetry arrives through the same SNS/SQS pipeline into our MSK cluster, reaches the data lake the same way as the streaming platform (case 02), and is fed to the fleet product through an API.
What I don’t claim
The 40% downtime result was measured on log delivery. Streaming telemetry is launching, so I don’t attribute results to it yet.

The engineers already knew what was wrong. That knowledge just needed to travel faster than the batch schedule allowed.

Case 02 · Data platform

IoT Streaming Platform

Moving 5TB+ of daily telemetry from batch to streaming, and everything that became possible once we did.

Live · HIPAA-compliant
The story

At 5TB+ a day, batch had become the ceiling on everything: machine-learning models couldn’t run on fresh data, remote diagnostics worked hours behind, and onboarding a new team onto the data took around four months. The platform needed a new foundation, HIPAA-compliant throughout, and open to products that didn’t exist yet.

I started with service and remote engineers to find where latency actually changed a decision. Most needed data in minutes, not milliseconds, and that shaped both what we built and how we measured it.

North StarThe share of critical use cases with data available in under five minutes. Not infrastructure latency; what the engineer actually experiences. The highest-value use cases went first, and the time-critical ones are still heading for under five.
My role

Owned the vision and roadmap. Defined the North Star, matched the architecture and technology choice to what users needed, prioritized which use cases to onboard first, and tracked adoption, freshness, reliability and business impact.

Swipe the diagram sideways →
Fig 02 — Devices to Kafka to processing, then three storage tiers that products read from

Data flows in on the left and is processed once. Fresh data goes to a near-real-time layer for alerts and dashboards, recent data to a fast store that apps query, and older data to cheap long-term storage.

Key decision — Amazon Kinesis vs. MSK (Kafka)

Considered: Amazon Kinesis

Met the minutes-level need, but shard-based throughput costs were expected to climb at 5TB+/day.

Chosen: MSK (Kafka)

Also met the need, and won on replayability, fit with our AWS architecture and the Kafka ecosystem we already had.

AlsoPySpark over Flink: Flink expertise was scarce, while a trained PySpark pool made the platform easier to maintain and staff.

Under the hoodfor the technical reader
Ingest
Kafka on AWS MSK ingests 5TB+ a day. Incoming formats (JSON, XML, TXT, CSV and raw logs) are standardized to JSON with topic schemas in Glue Schema Registry.
Process
Spark on EMR with PySpark transforms the stream. A near-real-time layer stores data in chunks; selected anomaly use cases also go to Elasticsearch, where watchers monitor for anomalies and trigger model workflows.
Hot store
RDS, holding about 60 days of near-real-time data with weekly archival. It serves APIs for consuming apps and operational analytics.
Cold store
S3 in Parquet with a medallion architecture, queried through Athena: five years, monthly archival, for latency-insensitive use cases. Glue prepares historical data for discovery and analytics.
Compliance
The platform carries PHI (protected health information), which is why it is built to be HIPAA-compliant.

The product was never “a Kafka platform”. It was faster access to machine intelligence, so engineers could make decisions sooner.

Case 03 · GenAI product

GenAI Conversational Assistant

The answers existed, in telemetry, service manuals and training material. Engineers just had to search each source and interpret it by hand.

MVP · engineer stays in the loop
The story

Service and remote engineers had plenty of machine telemetry and technical documentation, but getting an answer meant searching several sources and interpreting the result manually. The data wasn’t the bottleneck; the last mile of understanding was.

The assistant brings those sources together and answers in plain language, and the engineer still makes the call. In the MVP, diagnostic response time fell by about 60%, from roughly an hour to about 24 minutes. Since then, requests to extend it have kept coming in.

My role

Led product work from use-case definition and MVP scope through validation with engineering and the target users, and helped define the metadata that lets the assistant understand machine parameters.

Swipe the diagram sideways →
Fig 03 — Documents and machine data, grounded by metadata; the engineer decides

Three inputs feed one assistant: documents, machine data, and a description of what each machine parameter means. The assistant answers; the engineer decides, and tells us whether the answer helped.

Key decision — raw data columns vs. semantic metadata

Option A: raw columns

Hand the model the database fields as they are. Quick to wire up, but a column name says nothing about what tube temperature, pressure or flow rate mean, so answers come out generic and engineers won’t trust them.

Chosen: semantic metadata

Describe every parameter in metadata the model can read, so it interprets machine data the way an engineer would: specific enough to act on, and to ask follow-ups against.

AlsoRetrieval (RAG) instead of retraining the model each time the knowledge changes.
Guardrails & evaluation

The assistant recommends; it never acts on a machine or makes a service decision. We capture whether each recommendation was followed and whether it resolved the issue. I judged the product at three levels: did it retrieve the right information, was the answer accurate and grounded, and did engineers resolve issues faster.

Under the hoodfor the technical reader
Structured data
Machine data lives in RDS, with Neptune representing relationships between entities.
Documents (RAG)
Service manuals and training material sit in S3. Content is chunked and embedded, and relevant passages are retrieved with Amazon S3 Vectors.
Orchestration
For each question, orchestration pulls the relevant machine information and document passages together and passes that context to the LLM: GPT in the MVP, with Mistral evaluated as another option.
Feedback loop
Engineers’ feedback records whether a recommendation was followed and whether it resolved the issue.
Boundaries
No model was trained from scratch, and the assistant has no direct control over machines.

The hardest part wasn’t the model. It was teaching it what our parameters mean, so that asking beat searching every source yourself.

Case 04 · AI agents

AI Agents across the Software Lifecycle

Too much product time went into translating requirements into stories, then checking finished work against acceptance criteria. Two agents now take the first pass at both.

Pilot complete · now in adoption
The story

Our product knowledge lived in Confluence, in Git, and in years of requirements and user stories in Rally. Turning a new requirement into well-structured engineering stories meant translating all of that by hand, and checking delivered work against the acceptance criteria was manual too.

I designed and piloted two agents on AWS Kiro: a Product Agent that drafts the story with that full context, and an Acceptance Agent that checks finished work against the criteria. In the pilot, we measured completion times before and after and saw 50–70% time saved across the lifecycle. It’s now being rolled out.

My role

Designed, built and piloted both agents; defined the workflows worth improving, shaped the agents and their knowledge sources with engineering, and set the human-in-the-loop model.

Swipe the diagram sideways →
Fig 04 — Two agents at two points in the lifecycle; people keep the judgment calls

Top row: a requirement becomes a drafted story and engineers build it. Bottom row: the Acceptance Agent checks the work criterion by criterion, clears the objective ones, and sends the subjective ones to a person.

Key decision — let the agent accept everything, or keep a person in the loop

Considered: fully autonomous acceptance

Fastest on paper. But an agent independently accepting every change puts speed ahead of judgment, and nobody is accountable when it gets one wrong.

Chosen: agent clears the objective, a person decides the rest

The agent checks each criterion and can approve objective, low-risk ones. Subjective quality checks go to a person, and human approvals are recorded in the story discussion.

Under the hoodfor the technical reader
Product Agent
Takes a new requirement as a Markdown file or Excel context. Through MCP it reads past stories, Confluence pages and Git content, then generates a Confluence page and a Rally user story. Guardrails cover required fields, document upload, story format and acceptance criteria.
Build step
Engineers select and prioritize the story, implement it with Kiro, and push the code to Git.
Acceptance Agent
Reviews acceptance criteria one by one. Objective, low-risk checks can be approved by the agent; subjective quality checks are routed to a person, whose approval is recorded in the discussion section for traceability.
Boundaries
No model fine-tuning. The agents work from retrieved reference material and guardrails.

The goal isn’t AI-written stories. It’s less repetitive work across the lifecycle, with people still responsible wherever judgment or risk is involved.

Case 05 · Product discovery

Probe Intelligence

Replacing ultrasound probes is a significant cost. Three signals could show where it can be avoided.

In solutioning · discovery complete · nothing built yet
The story

I’m directing a 10-member team to shape a probe intelligence product around three questions about every ultrasound probe: Is it genuine? How has it been used? Is it under- or over-utilized?

I led discovery and problem identification, then sized the opportunity at about $50M of projected cost savings, from the average number of probes replaced and what each replacement costs. That is a sized opportunity, not a delivered saving.

My role

Set the product vision and scope, led discovery and the sizing, and lead the team day to day. The team helps with analysis and implementation.
